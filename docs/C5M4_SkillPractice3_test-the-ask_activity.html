Test the ask

An explicit decision request is a named statement of what you are asking a reader to approve, who approves it, and by when. Decision-makers approve specific things. A paper that hands them a specific thing can get a decision. A paper that leaves the ask implied goes back to its author, because the reader found nothing in it to approve.

An advisor's case for an AI assistant that helps a manufacturer's plant inspectors catch defects went to the investment committee and came back without a decision.

The finance partner found three approvals inside it: funding for the first phase, a three-year agreement with the software provider, and two inspector positions moved into the quality function's plan. Below are seven sentences from the advisor's drafts of the ask.

Hold each sentence against three parts: what is to be approved, who approves it, and by when.

Complete ask: all three parts, for one approval.

Missing a part: the what, the who, or the when is absent.

Two decisions: the sentence joins two approvals.

Three approvals, three sentences

The provider agreement and the two positions each need a sentence of their own, with their own approver and their own date. The committee can then grant one and refuse another without sending the whole paper back.

Second pass: move the authority

The who in an ask has to be the body that can grant it. Suppose the investment committee may approve spending up to a limit, and anything larger goes to the executive committee. Move the limit and watch the two complete sentences. Nothing here is checked.
