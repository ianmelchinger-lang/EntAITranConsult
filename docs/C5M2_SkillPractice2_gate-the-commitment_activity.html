Gate the commitment

Staged investment turns one large commitment into a sequence of smaller decisions. Each decision sits at a gate, a point where the organization can expand, hold, or stop. Staging pays when the outcome is still unresolved, because information arrives over time and a later decision can use it. A gate is only as good as the observation it waits for.

A manufacturer’s service function asks for $264,000 at signing to put a drafting assistant in all seven of its service teams. Finance has split the request into three tranches, which are portions of the money released one at a time.

The first tranche, $48,000 at signing, funds two teams for a quarter. A gate at month three releases $96,000 to extend to all seven teams. A gate at month nine releases $120,000 for the rest of a three-year licence. All amounts here are illustrative.

Six candidate conditions follow. Place each at the first gate where it is both in hand and able to change the next tranche. If a condition could never change a decision, mark it as not a gate condition.

Second pass: read the month-three gate

Finance wrote the gate’s rule before the quarter began. Expand if a year of net saving at seven teams covers the $96,000 the gate releases. Stop if the reread work is worth as much as the whole claimed saving. Otherwise hold at two teams and measure another quarter.

The claim is 4,100 hours a year: 16,400 drafts at 15 minutes saved each. A full reread takes the quality group 20 minutes. The model prices the moved work at the same $46 an hour as the saving. Move the measured reread share and read the gate. Nothing here is checked.
