A multinational manufacturer holds its quarterly funding review. Five AI lines are on the table, listed in the delivery plan by start date. The discretionary pool, the part of the budget not already committed, covers three of them, because most of the technology budget is already spoken for by keeping existing systems running.

Two lines have results. Visual inspection has run as a pilot at one plant and produced a measured result. Drafting supplier nonconformance write-ups has run for ninety days at three plants, and the time per write-up fell from 38 minutes to 22. Two lines have not started. The demand-forecasting model for all plants has been requested for two years, and its sponsor projects fewer days of inventory. The case-management system planned for next year has no users yet, and its planning notes assume the people who will use it are already comfortable working with AI.

The fifth line is the company-wide assistant, in its first year: 3,400 seats at $34 per seat per month, or a year, with no stated return. The sponsor has stopped asking which of the five are worth having. All five are. The question is which three get money this quarter.
